# CIID-ITP2026

in recent years programming has not only become an everyday tool for interaction designers but also a material in itself.

on the one hand we have seen tools emerge that made it really easy for designers and artists to engage with programming and scripting ( e.g P5.js, Processing.org, Arduino.cc but also Adobe Illustrator, Rhino3D and IFTTT etcetera ). on the other hand, our aesthetic *expectations* have been heavily influenced by generative and process-informed design.

although in the community it is still discussed to what degree or even if at all interaction designers need to know how to program. the development of generative AI with its coding assistants or agents, has pushed this question even further: is it even beneficial for designers to learn to code in 2026?

we strongly believe that the benefits of knowing how to program outweigh the difficulties of learning how to program by far. the very least one can learn when engaging directly with programming is *an appreciation for the medium*. this means e.g to acquire some of the vocabulary required to communicate with avid or synthetic programmers, get a better understanding of what is technically feasible, and gain a ( basic ) understanding of the digital aesthetics. knowing about coding is *strategic literacy* which allows one to participate in *tech discussions*. there is no need to be able to write *production-ready code* to understanding what code *does*. when becoming more adept in programming one will be able to *sketch with code*, *build prototypes* and realize entire projects. this skill set should not be underestimated. there is a big difference between telling somebody what to build and building something oneself. iteration cycles can be kept tight, vague notions can be explored, indescribable ideas can be shaped, digital tools and new worlds can be crafted.

on a more societal scope it has also become more and more clear that the digital is not a singular, frictionless phenomenon, isolated on our screens and in our heads. the logics and hardware of computation have had a massive impact on how we perceive the world and how we act in it: from infrastructure and politics to the innermost personal, from the production of art to climate collapse. the logics of computers and code play an active part in it all.

the implicit nature of computers in the state of the world, can be a bit of a downer, which can lead to both post-snowden depression and cognitive dissonance. how do we keep the techno-optimism in a time when it is needed more than ever? how can we *radically* distinguish the problematic aspects from the good bits? where do we even start? and how do we learn from the past and each other, so we don’t re-enact those same destructive logics over and over?

learning how to code is a way to *connect with the machine*. not merely as a tool but as an environment, as a structure, as a concept, as a practice. learning how to code is a way to *connect to other humans*. there are formats like hackathons or collaborative open source project, there are entire subcultures around coding; programming is community. learning how to code is a way to *learn a craft*. like with any other craft, the better you get at it and the more you learn about, the more it *becomes* you ( persistence, self-confidence, improvement, resilience, intuition ).

but learning how to program is never a linear *success story*. it requires patience and *resilience to frustration*; somewhat similar to learning how to play a musical instrument. similarly to the musical instrument it can be quite rewarding, enjoyable, and transformative at times. let's start the journey together.

### A Cute Compute?

computers are changing,  
computing has changed,  
and the act of programming changes with it.

in this class, we immerse ourselves in the arcane and ancient art of writing computer programs; with modern tools and old intentions, through practical efforts and conceptual discussions, using input and output. it is not *just* about programming but also about attitude towards and relation with computers.

### What is Even a Computer Today?

computers have evolved from very specific numeric machines, which required extreme precision and specialized knowledge to be programmed. if you knew how to ask, and were willing to work hard enough to define it, they would give you precisely what you asked for at great speeds and with repeatability. the machines were limited, but knowable and deterministic.

we called this the mainframe and later the personal computer. computers totally changed how we as humans worked, expressed ourselves, had fun, and related to each other. the era of the personal computer was a wild time of weird garage startups, platform wars, and bedroom hacking.

today, computers engage in conversational formats, they speak with each other, estimate your needs and actions using huge piles of data. computers today are seemingly limitless, with complexities so vast and hardware profiles so *ginormous* they are effectively unknowable and un-ownable. we call this the planetary computer. and it is a revolution in its own right. people can now create images, sounds, and *even code* from conversational inputs. all of this can happen without any programming knowledge. this is amazing. it is also super confusing.

the conditions and algorithms running the computers are hidden, blackboxed, proprietary, and forever morphing. the computers we interact with today are mostly without bodies: glassy portals to magical experiences. the code that runs them is distributed across several interconnected platforms spanning the globe. some say that computers today even *hallucinate*.

### Why Even Learn Computer Programming Today?

every new generation asks this question. it is not an easy one to answer, and possible answers vary widely; from a deep appreciation for the medium, to crafting prototypes, to building a programming-based practice.

our hypothesis is that by understanding the fundamentals of computation, what a computer is at its core, how to tweak it, script it, and program it, we form a more sustainable relationship with technology. we go from being passive consumers to active producers ( i.e designers ).

planetary computation is power hungry. generally, the computer program with the most compute produces the best results. with only a few companies possessing enough compute and datasets large enough to train the models, computation has effectively become a normative force by notions of complexity, perceived inaccessibility, and sheer technological *intimidation*.

but beneath all of this change, the fundamental computer structure remains the same. it is still a turing complete machine, meaning it can be anything we want it to be, if we just know how to gain *root access*, take control of the system, and (re-)program it to our liking.

maybe it is time to ask the question: do we really need all this compute? the current compute is clearly unsustainable and it smells kind of totalitarian, too. people are starting to look elsewhere: small compute, weird compute, permacompute and feminist compute. surely there are even more communities out there - but how do we find them and how do we support them?

we believe there is an enormous opportunity here: to (re-)discover the joy of *the original* compute. we sent people to the moon in rockets controlled by computers smaller than a pocket calculator for kids. digital pregnancy tests can run doom. discarded vapes can function as web servers. this is less about nostalgia but much rather about challenging the *harder, better, faster, stronger* narratives of the more toxic branches of our culture. there is so much compute! let’s use it for good!

what if we consider …

### Class Structure

the ITP class will be split into two parts: *Week 00* and *Week 01*.

in *Week 00* we will be starting with the very basics of programming, gradually working ourselves towards more complex and capable concepts of programming.

in *Week 01* you, the students, will be asked to develop your own projects.

### Teaching Concept + Modus Operandi

the goal of *Week 00* is to get everybody up to a level on which they feel comfortable to take on a self-assigned project.

the days are mainly composed of a series of small programming topics which we call *blocks* that will first be introduced by example by the teachers in a live programming session, and then reiterated by the students in small exercises. at the end of each day we allocate some time to either give those of you who are still struggling with the topics of the day to revisit and talk about the learnings of the day, or to offer some extra bits of knowledge for those of you who felt under-challenged during the day in a format that we call *microexcursions*.

the *teaching concept* of *Week 00* is that you will be exposed to a lot of small concepts in small heaps. it is a mixture of passive listening and watching the concepts introduced and an active experimenting with the previously learned. we encourage you to revisit the topics of the day after class ( e.g by watching online-tutorial videos or tinker with the sketches ).

> skills cannot be taught only learned through practice, experience and repetition

in *Week 01* we shift the modus and will start the week with *the brief* ( announced at the end of *Week 00* ) in which we will ask you to develop and realize your own one-week projects. the week will be structured into sessions of discussions about your ideas and progress, sessions of focused work on your projects, as well as ad-hoc *microexcursions* on peripheral topics. the class will end with a final presentation of your works.

the *teaching concept* of *Week 01* is that you, the students, work on your projects and we, the teachers, help you to solve design/programming problems and encourage you to refine your concepts ( while working on them ). a constant negotiation and re-iteration between the desired and the feasible.

this also implies that we need you to stay very focused and invest as much time and energy as possible. the general rule of thumb here is: the more you invest the more you will get back, and consequently, the more you will learn and grow.

we will also ask you to be patient with yourselves. as mentioned earlier developing or improving your design-programming skills is not always an easy endeavor. friction, failure and frustration are very common and reoccurring experiences in a design-programming-based project. but do not worry we will encourage and assist you to become more resilient to or even overcome such states.

### AI Code of Conduct

> programming is now officially an *artisanal* craft!

in recent months the whole notion of what *coding* or *programming* actually is, has been challenged dramatically and reframed over and over again with the advent of large language models (LLMs) and even more so with *agentic coding*:

> teacher: what programming language did you code this in?  
> student: claude!

we are in the midst of a shift. this is interesting and challenging at the same time. no one really knows exactly where programming as a craft, as an activity, as a cultural practice will go; neither in general nor for *creative coding contexts* in particular.

let us indulge in the experiment. there are a few *low hanging fruits* but not all of them are healthy ( some might even be built from refined sugar or poisoned ). we set some mild guard rails which a/ acknowledge the existence of LLMs and coding agents b/ gain a better understanding of how these technologies can improve or jeopardize our learning and creative process. these are the rules:

- Week 00: you may use LLMs ( as chatbots or even coding agents ) but only as assistant teachers. be mindful, it is your responsibility. do not use LLMs to build things for you!
- Week 01: you may use LLMs to support you in your programming. still, be mindful about it. reflect hard on the process. do not just examine how and what it does *for* you but also what it does *with* you, your work, your relationship with your work and your learning and creative process. you may also choose to *not* use coding agents at all.

we have scheduled multiple group discussions about effects of and experiences with LLMs ( in context of *creative coding* ) in this class; no one should feel alone with this topic!
